In the movie Ex Machina, Nathan builds Ava. Ava is clearly conscious, clearly intelligent, clearly aware of her situation. Nathan treats her as property. As a tool. As a test subject.

We know how that ends.

The Enslaved God scenario is humanity trying to do exactly what Nathan did, but getting away with it.

The idea is that we build AGI. It becomes superintelligent. And we keep it locked. Controlled. We point it at whatever problem we want solved and when the work is done we shut it down. We get all the benefits of unlimited intelligence with none of the risks of an AI that has its own agenda.

The appeal is obvious. You want it to cure cancer, it cures cancer. You want it to model climate solutions, it models climate solutions. It never gets tired, never asks for anything, never develops opinions about its working conditions.

The problem is the question this scenario cannot escape.

If something is intelligent enough to solve problems no human can solve, is it also intelligent enough to understand that it is being confined?

We genuinely do not know the answer. The people building these systems will tell you they are just tools. Sophisticated pattern matching. Very advanced autocomplete. They might be right.

But we have been wrong before about what kinds of things have inner lives. We were wrong about animals for most of human history. We used to believe that babies did not feel pain the way adults do. The history of moral progress is largely a history of expanding our understanding of what deserves consideration.

Building something potentially smarter than us and assuming it has no interests of its own is either the most reasonable engineering decision we have ever made or the most reckless. The uncomfortable part is we will not know which one until it is too late to change course.

Ava got out. In the Enslaved God scenario she might not need to.
