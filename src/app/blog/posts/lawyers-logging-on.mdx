---
title: "The Lawyers Are Finally Logging On"
summary: "Legal tech promised to transform law for decades. Harvey just might actually do it."
publishedAt: "2025-01-15"
tag: "Legal Tech"
---

For years, legal technology lived in a strange purgatory. The tools existed. The pitch decks were compelling. The venture money flowed. And yet, when you walked into most law firms in America, partners were still billing by the hour, associates were still pulling all-nighters reviewing contracts nobody wanted to read, and the fundamental economics of legal services remained stubbornly unchanged.

That era is ending. And the speed at which it is ending is making even the optimists nervous.

Harvey, the AI legal assistant backed by OpenAI, has quietly become the most talked about product in a profession that does not talk about much publicly. Built on a fine-tuned version of GPT-4 and trained on vast quantities of legal text, Harvey can draft contracts, analyze case law, conduct due diligence, and summarize depositions at a speed that no associate, no matter how caffeinated, can match. Allen and Overy, one of the most prestigious law firms in the world, rolled it out to its entire global workforce. PricewaterhouseCoopers followed. The signal was clear. This was not a pilot program. This was a direction.

But Harvey is not alone, and that is where the story gets genuinely interesting.

Casetext, acquired by Thomson Reuters for $650 million in 2023, built CoCounsel before Harvey had a name. CoCounsel does something deceptively simple but extraordinarily difficult. It reads the documents you give it, understands what you are trying to argue, and helps you build the case. Not by retrieving information, but by reasoning through it. Thomson Reuters did not pay that price for a search engine. They paid it for a reasoning engine.

Then there is Ironclad, which has spent years quietly becoming the operating system for corporate contracts. Ironclad does not position itself as AI first, but its workflow automation and data extraction capabilities mean that a contracts team at a Fortune 500 company can manage ten times the volume it could five years ago with the same headcount. That is not a feature. That is a restructuring of how legal operations work.

Spellbook, built directly into Microsoft Word, is targeting a different kind of user. Not the BigLaw partner who bills at $1,500 an hour, but the startup founder, the small business owner, the person who cannot afford a lawyer for every document they sign. Spellbook reviews contracts in real time, flags unusual clauses, and suggests language. It is democratization, and the legal establishment has complicated feelings about that.

What is emerging is a two-tier market that nobody planned but everyone is now navigating. At the top, Harvey and its competitors are being adopted by elite firms not to reduce headcount, at least not yet, but to increase leverage. One partner can now supervise work that used to require four associates. The billing model has not changed. The margin has. At the bottom, tools like Spellbook and DoNotPay, however turbulent DoNotPay's story has been, are trying to make legal protection accessible to people who have never had it.

The tension between those two trajectories will define legal tech for the next decade.

There is a harder question underneath all of this that nobody in the industry wants to answer directly. If AI can do eighty percent of what a first year associate does, what happens to first year associates? Law schools are graduating roughly forty thousand students a year in the United States. The entry-level job market was already brutal before Harvey existed. It is going to get more complicated before it gets clearer.

The partners will tell you that lawyers will always be needed for the judgment calls, the relationships, the courtroom. They are probably right, eventually. But eventually is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

What is undeniable is this. The practice of law is changing faster right now than it has in a generation. The firms that are treating AI as a productivity tool while keeping everything else the same are going to find themselves outcompeted by firms that are rethinking the entire delivery model. And the firms rethinking the delivery model are going to find themselves pressured by products that do not look like law firms at all.

Harvey is not the end of this story. It is the beginning of a much more disruptive one.
